Chapter 1 · Geometry & regularization

The geometry of a rocking body

Before solving the dynamics, define what it means for a rigid body to rock: its potential energy, its moving tipping point, and the smooth approximation that makes the equations numerically usable.

01
Two-dimensional motivation

Rocking is a corner-to-corner motion

Consider a rectangular rigid body rocking in two dimensions. When the body rocks to the right, θ > 0; when it rocks to the left, θ < 0. The potential energy changes according to which corner carries the body.

With r₀ the distance from a corner to the center of mass and θ₀ the geometric angle, the two branches can be written as one expression:

V(θ) = mg√(r₀² + h²) sin(θ₀ + |θ|)

For small oscillations this becomes V(θ) ≈ mgh + mgr₀ sin(|θ|). The absolute value captures the physical switch between the two supports.

02

Replace the sharp switch without losing the geometry

The absolute value and the associated sign function are convenient descriptions of a perfectly flat contact surface, but they are not differentiable at the upright position. A differentiable approximation can be interpreted physically as a slight convexity at the interface.

03
The C³ approximation

Regularization is part of the model

The tipping point is defined by the sign of the rocking angle:

r(q) = r₀ sgn(θ) (cos φ, sin φ, 0)ᵀ

For θ < 0, the tipping point is diametrically opposite. The identity r(φ, −θ) = r(φ + π, θ) lets the dynamics use one uniform description instead of separate if–then branches.

The replacement for sgn is required to be constant at −1 and +1 outside a width w, with a smooth curve between them. C³ continuity matters because derivatives enter the velocity, kinetic energy, mass-matrix derivatives, and Newton iterations in an implicit ODE solver. A function such as tanh is not equivalent: without constant branches, pure rocking never occurs.

04
Geometric derivation

Turn the contact angle into a curve

The smooth sign determines the horizontal position of the tipping point, while the contact angle determines the local tangent of the body. Together they give the plotted geometry:

r(θ) = r₀ sgn(θ)
dy/dr = tan(θ)
dy/dθ = r₀ sgn′(θ) tan(θ)

The last relation is the chain rule: the tipping point moves with sgn′(θ), and the vertical height changes according to the local slope tan(θ). The curve is mirrored about the origin, so positive and negative rocking share the same construction.

Outside the smooth transition, sgn′(θ) = 0. The horizontal coordinate then stays fixed at r = ±r₀ while y continues to change, which is why the geometric path becomes vertical on the side walls.
